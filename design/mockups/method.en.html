Method

The same rules apply to everyone on record, whatever their country or party. We describe outcomes; we do not judge intentions.

What we record

A promise is a statement about the future that commits to an action or a result that can be checked. We record it only from the person’s or party’s own published material — a platform, a speech, an interview they published — quoted word for word, with the page or paragraph.

We also record predictions: a statement that something will happen by a given time, including judgments about where an industry or a trend is heading. Promises and predictions follow the same rules and appear side by side; each item says which it is.

We do not record slogans, statements of values, descriptions of the present or criticism of others. Within a platform, every statement that fits this definition is recorded. Importance and ease of checking are not reasons to leave one out.

When the words were said in another language, the page shows them translated into the page’s language, with the original words directly underneath, labelled “Original (French)” or “Original (English)”. The original is never hidden and never paraphrased.

Measurable, vague and hedged

Measurable
A result that can be measured, with a deadline. The criteria for “came true” are written down when the item is recorded, before the outcome is known, and are not changed afterwards. If they have to be corrected, the change is public.
Vague
A clear direction without a measurable result (“a greener town”). Recorded; always “Unverifiable”.
Hedged
Qualified by words such as “we will endeavour to”. Recorded; always “Unverifiable”.

Deadlines

When the promise gives a deadline, we use it. When a campaign promise gives none, the deadline is the end of the term being sought, and the page says “Deadline inferred: end of term”.

The deadline matters differently for each outcome:

  • “Came true” and “Partly came true” can be given before the deadline, as soon as the documents show the result.
  • “Did not come true” is given only once the deadline has passed, or earlier if the person publicly gives up the promise; their own words are then the evidence.
  • Otherwise, until the deadline the verdict is “Pending”.

The six verdicts

A promise and a prediction are the same kind of thing: words said in the past about a future that may or may not happen. They share six verdicts, all shown in the same way; none is a mark of praise or blame. Every verdict except “Pending” comes with a short explanation.

Unverifiable
Too vague or too hedged to be checked, or it depended on a condition that never arose. The explanation says which.
Pending
Not due yet, and no result to record yet.
To be assessed
Due, but the documents are not enough to decide yet. The page always says where it is stuck and what is missing.
Came true
The criteria written when the item was recorded were met in full.
Partly came true
The criteria were met in part. When the result can be counted, the explanation gives the figures: what was promised and what the documents show (“fibre for every home promised by 2025; 90.5% of premises at the end of 2025”).
Did not come true
Due, and the criteria were not met; or the person publicly gave it up before the deadline.

We judge results, not effort: a bill that was introduced but not passed does not make a promise come true.

What counts as evidence

We look at the evidence, not at who is speaking. A verdict on an outcome needs one official document, or two independent sources from different publishers. A statement — by the person, a government, an opponent or a commentator — is a lead to a document, never evidence in itself.

Kinds of source and whether they can support a verdict
Kind of sourceCan it support a verdict?
PlatformNo. It is where the promise comes from.
Statement: speech, press release, interview; a public body’s assessment of its own workNo. A lead only.
Public renunciation: the person’s own public words giving up a promiseOnly for “Did not come true” on that promise. One is enough.
Law, official gazette, official statistics, budget document, parliamentary record, court decision, public audit report, other official document that records rather than assessesYes. One is enough.
News report based on the outlet’s own reporting; study with published dataYes, when two different publishers each establish the fact.

Two outlets count as two only if each established the fact itself. One outlet reprinting another, or two outlets using the same agency story, count as one. A report that only relays what someone said is a statement.

A government press release saying a promise has been fulfilled is a statement. We look for the law, budget or data it refers to and cite that instead.

Copies of documents

When a document is recorded, we keep a copy of the file, its original address, the date it was retrieved and its SHA-256 fingerprint. The copies are not published. Each page shows the original address and the fingerprint, so anyone holding a copy can check that it is the same file, even if the original page later changes or disappears.

Who decides

  1. A jury of at least two AI models from different companies makes a first assessment. Each works alone and cannot see the others’ answers.
  2. A neutrality check looks at the choice of material, the wording and the evidence.
  3. A person checks the verdict and the evidence, and confirms it. Nothing is published without this step.

Each item shows the date it was confirmed by a person, then the jury’s votes as they were recorded, including any disagreement. We keep every vote so that the agreement between the jury and the person can be measured over time.

Corrections and withdrawals

Anyone can ask for a correction by writing to corrections@example.org. Please give the item number.

Nothing published is overwritten. When a verdict changes, the earlier verdict and both dates stay visible on the item’s page and on the Corrections page. When an item is withdrawn, its address stays and shows the date and the reason; the withdrawn content is no longer displayed.
